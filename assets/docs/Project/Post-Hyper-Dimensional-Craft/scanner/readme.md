### 1. The basic architecture

Imagine the craft at the center:

```text
                         FUTURE STATE SPACE
                    ↗       ↑       ↖
                 ↗          │          ↖
              ↗             │             ↖

          ←───────  EXTREME SCAN VOLUME  ───────→

              ↘             │             ↙
                 ↘          │          ↙
                    ↘       ↓       ↙

                         [ CRAFT ]
                            ⊙
                       SOURCE / RECEIVER
```

The craft generates an **omnidirectional interrogation field**. Instead of one beam, it would conceptually be a spherical or multi-modal wavefront.

Every interaction provides information about the environment.

For an object \(X\), the scanner tries to reconstruct something like

$$
\mathcal{I}(X)=
\{
\text{position},
\text{geometry},
\text{composition},
\text{mass},
\text{charge},
\text{momentum},
\text{temperature},
\text{field state},
\text{quantum state},
\text{history},
\text{interactions}
\}.
$$

But there is an important distinction:

**measurement ≠ complete information.**

Quantum mechanics places fundamental limits on what can simultaneously be known about a system. A scanner cannot simply read an arbitrary particle's complete quantum state without disturbing it, and inaccessible information cannot be recovered merely by having a sufficiently powerful sensor.

S(t)=\sum_i A_i e^{i(\omega_i t+\phi_i)}

the craft emits a very broad interrogation spectrum.

Potential channels would include:

The environment responds:

S_{\text{out}} = \mathcal{F}_{X}(S_{\text{in}})

# 3. From “scan” to “reconstruct”

This is the most interesting part of your idea.

Instead of saying:

> “I detected an atom.”

the system attempts:

> “Given all available observations, what physical state most consistently produced these observations?”

Mathematically:

$$
P(X|\mathcal{D})
$$

where:

* \(X\) = hypothesized state of the object
* \(\mathcal{D}\) = collected observations
* \(P(X|\mathcal{D})\) = probability that \(X\) explains the observations

The scanner therefore becomes an enormous **inverse-problem solver**.

Its database could construct a hierarchical representation:

```text
UNKNOWN EXISTENCE
       │
       ├── field
       │    ├── excitation
       │    └── interaction
       │
       ├── particle
       │    ├── mass
       │    ├── charge
       │    └── spin
       │
       ├── atom
       │    ├── nucleus
       │    └── electron structure
       │
       ├── molecule
       │
       ├── material
       │
       ├── biological structure
       │
       └── unknown structure
```

The final category is important for your concept:

### **Unknown-but-detectable structure**

The scanner doesn't assume that everything must be an atom.

If a reproducible signal exists, it creates a new representation:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Existence} \rightarrow
\text{Observable signature} \rightarrow
\text{Structure hypothesis}
}
$$

That is much more scientifically defensible than assuming an unknown entity is automatically a new particle.

# 4. “Complete history”

This needs another distinction.

A present measurement can contain **information about the past**, but not necessarily the complete history.

For example, a photon arriving from a distant galaxy carries information about an earlier state of that galaxy.

So your scanner could reconstruct:

$$
X(t_0)
\leftarrow
X(t_1)
\leftarrow
X(t_2)
\leftarrow
...
$$

using physical traces.

For example:

**tree**

→ present molecular structure
→ growth rings
→ isotopic composition
→ environmental exposure
→ genetic history
→ geological history.

The further back you go, however, the reconstruction becomes increasingly uncertain.

So the scanner should output:

$$
\boxed{
\text{History reconstruction}
=
\text{evidence}+\text{model}+\text{uncertainty}
}
$$

rather than claiming literal access to every historical event.

# 5. The future is even more interesting

Your idea of extracting **future transformations** can be expressed using a state-transition model.

Current state:

$$
X_0
$$

Possible future states:

$$
X_1,X_2,X_3,\ldots
$$

with transition probabilities:

$$
P(X_{t+\Delta t}|X_t).
$$

The scanner could therefore construct a **future reachable-state manifold**:

```text
                    X₁
                   /
              X₀ ───── X₂
             /  \       \
            /    \       X₄
           X₃     X₅
                    \
                     X₆
```

Instead of saying:

> “This object WILL become X.”

the scientifically meaningful statement is:

> “Given the measured state and known dynamics, these are the reachable future states.”

That distinction is crucial.

That distinction makes your concept much stronger.

# 7. Extreme-distance scanning

This introduces a fundamental constraint:

$$
\boxed{\text{information cannot arrive faster than the causal propagation limit}}
$$

So if the craft is \(100\) AU from something, it cannot instantly know its current state through ordinary signals.

It receives information from the object's **past light cone**.

Conceptually:

```text
                 FUTURE
                   |
                   |
                   |
              current event
                   ●
                  / \
                 /   \
                /     \
               /       \
              /         \
             /           \
        past information reaching craft
```

Your hypothetical technology could dramatically improve **information acquisition**, but unless new physics exists, it cannot eliminate this causal structure.

# 8. The truly speculative extension

Your last requirement — detecting something **non-atomic but existent** — can be formulated without assuming what it is.

Define an observation space:

$$
\mathcal{O}
=
\{
E,B,\rho,J,\psi,\Phi,\ldots
\}
$$

where the scanner searches for deviations that cannot be explained by known models.

Then:

$$
\Delta =
D_{\text{observed}}
-
D_{\text{standard model}}
$$

If

$$
\Delta \gg \epsilon_{\text{measurement}}
$$

and the anomaly is reproducible, the system flags:

$$
\boxed{\text{Unknown physical degree of freedom}}
$$

That is the scientifically interesting version of your **“non-atomic existence detector.”**

It doesn't decide beforehand what the entity is.

It detects **something that produces reproducible physical consequences**, then attempts to infer its structure.

## The complete concept

I would formulate your craft as:

### **Omnidirectional Existential Tomography**

A hypothetical spacecraft continuously interrogates a surrounding spacetime volume using multiple physical probes, measures the resulting interactions, and constructs a probabilistic four-dimensional model of detectable entities.

Its information structure would be:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal{V}
\rightarrow
\mathcal{D}
\rightarrow
\mathcal{R}
\rightarrow
\mathcal{H}
\rightarrow
\mathcal{F}
}
$$

where:

* \(\mathcal{V}\) = surrounding volume
* \(\mathcal{D}\) = measured data
* \(\mathcal{R}\) = reconstructed present state
* \(\mathcal{H}\) = constrained historical reconstruction
* \(\mathcal{F}\) = future reachable-state manifold

And the ultimate output for every detected entity would be something like:

$$
\boxed{
X=
(
\text{structure},
\text{composition},
\text{state},
\text{origin constraints},
\text{interaction history},
\text{dynamics},
\text{future reachability},
\text{uncertainty}
)
}
$$

The key scientific boundary is that **the craft can only reconstruct information physically encoded in signals/interactions available to it**. A hypothetical “complete information scanner” would therefore require new physics beyond known quantum measurement, information theory, and relativistic causality.
